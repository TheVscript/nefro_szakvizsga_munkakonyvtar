Approach to Hematuria: Core Curriculum 2026
Megan Prochaska, Luke F. Reynolds, and Anna Zisman
Hematuria is a frequent clinical finding in primary care, nephrology, and urology clinics. Accurate 
diagnosis after a positive dipstick requires confirmation with urine microscopy, with the American 
Urological Association (AUA) guidelines defining significant microscopic hematuria as ≥3 red 
blood cells per high-power field. Hematuria warrants careful evaluation to distinguish between 
glomerular and nonglomerular etiologies, with attention to risk factors for malignancy to ensure 
timely diagnosis. Recent updates to the AUA guidelines emphasize risk stratification, advocating 
tailored diagnostic approaches to optimize detection of urologic cancers while limiting unnec­
essary procedures, health care costs, and patient harm. This review covers the evaluation and 
management of hematuria, including risk profiling for malignancy. Evidence-based recommenda­
tions are provided for common clinical scenarios such as patients with hematuria from malignancy, 
kidney stones, and glomerular disease. This core curriculum guides clinicians through the 
nuanced decision making required in the diagnosis and management of hematuria, with reference 
to the latest guidelines.
Introduction
Hematuria, the presence of red blood cells 
(RBCs) in the urine, is characterized as either 
gross hematuria that is visible to the naked eye or 
microscopic hematuria, which is detectable only 
with urine microscopy. Hematuria is common in 
primary care, nephrology, and urology clinics, 
making up about 1 in 5 urology evaluations.
When a patient reports hematuria, the first 
step is to confirm that the urinary system is the 
source of the blood. A urine dipstick includes 
an indicator pad impregnated with reagents 
that undergo a color change in the presence of 
heme or myoglobin. Thus, a positive dipstick 
can result from the presence of RBCs but also 
from free hemoglobin or myoglobin. Blood 
loss from other sources (eg, the gynecologic 
tract), the presence of systemic conditions 
such as rhabdomyolysis (myoglobinuria), and 
the ingestion of pigments from certain foods 
or substances (eg, rifampin or beets) can be 
mistaken for hematuria or even produce false 
positives on urine dipstick (eg, rhabdomyol­
ysis). Vitamin C also can cause a false negative. 
For this reason, microscopic hematuria detec­
ted by dipstick requires a follow-up urine 
microscopy examination to confirm the diag­
nosis. After excluding the aforementioned 
conditions and confirming that the source of 
the RBCs is the urinary tract, the clinician 
should confirm the degree of hematuria. The 
American 
Urological 
Association 
(AUA) 
guidelines define microhematuria as ≥3 RBCs 
per high-power field on a single and properly 
collected urine specimen. If this definition is 
met, the hematuria is substantial enough to 
warrant additional work-up.
Once confirmed, hematuria demands an 
initial evaluation including a thorough history 
and physical examination with measurement 
of blood pressure and serum creatinine. Dur­
ing the history taking, one should consider the 
broad differential diagnosis of microscopic 
hematuria (Table 1) to focus the history on 
reviewing risk factors and signs of the po­
tential etiologies. For example, one should 
inquire about symptoms of infection, a history 
of recent trauma or urologic instrumentation, 
and symptoms of kidney stones or prostate 
conditions. Genitourinary cancer is the most 
concerning cause of hematuria, so specific 
consideration of cancer risk factors such as 
age, biological sex, and extensive smoking 
history is mandatory (Box 1).
The clinician should also evaluate clinical 
factors that can help distinguish glomerular 
and nonglomerular etiologies for hematuria. 
For example, a family history of Alport syn­
drome or a recent upper respiratory infection, 
which can be seen with IgA nephropathy, can 
provide crucial clues to direct the work-up to­
ward considering a glomerular cause. Urine 
microscopy can also provide important diag­
nostic clues. Findings of dysmorphic RBCs and 
RBC casts, the presence of additional abnor­
malities in the urine dipstick (ie, albumin­
uria), evidence of acute kidney injury, blood 
pressure elevations, and other systemic symp­
toms may indicate a glomerular cause. How­
ever, suspicion of glomerular bleeding should 
not negate a urologic work-up for most pa­
tients. Furthermore, given the smaller vol­

mizing referrals so that patients at highest risk for malig­

that opportunities for early detection are frequently 
missed. For example, one study noted that only 23% of 
patients with hematuria and risk factors for malignancy 
received appropriate imaging work-up and fewer (13%) 
underwent cystoscopy. Furthermore, certain patient 
populations are more likely to experience delay or not 
undergo a hematuria work-up compared with other pop­
ulations (ie, women, African American patients).
The intention of the guideline was to reduce such delays 
and the underevaluation of patients at high risk for ma­
lignancy while limiting the risks and costs associated with 
overevaluation of patients at low risk. For this reason, the 
guidelines recommend using a risk stratification system to 
guide evaluation after the initial assessment. The risk 
stratification was developed from a systematic review of 
the urothelial cancer risk factor literature, and it was 
subsequently validated in 3 separate studies. We re-create

Risk-Stratification Guides Work-Up
Risk stratification is a crucial first step because this will 
guide the appropriate work-up. The guidelines recom­
mend that low-risk patients be monitored with a repeat 
urinalysis in 6 months. If hematuria remains persistent 
then patients are reclassified to a higher level of risk. 
Intermediate-risk patients are recommended for cystos­
copy and kidney ultrasound, and high-risk patients are 
recommended for cystoscopy and upper urinary tract axial 
imaging with delayed phase imaging such as CT or mag­
netic resonance imaging.

Bladder Cancer
Bladder cancer, the 10th most common cancer worldwide 
and in the United States, is predominantly urothelial car­
cinoma. Bladder cancer is more common in men over the 
age of 65 years. The most common risk factors include 
smoking and occupational risk factors such as aromatic 
amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Any prior 
smoking has a population attributable risk of 0.50 in men 
and 0.52 in women. Smoking cessation can reduce the risk 
of bladder cancer, but their risk remains higher than for 
nonsmokers; secondhand smoke exposure also increases 
risk. Other less common but described risk factors include 
human papilloma virus infection, recurrent urinary in­
fections, Schistosoma haematobium infection, chronic inflam­
mation (chronic indwelling catheters), cyclophosphamide 
exposure, and type 2 diabetes.
As with other cancers, genetic factors can directly and 
indirectly affect bladder cancer risk. Prior genomewide 
association studies used 24 independent markers to 
developed a polygenic risk score that is associated with 
increased risk of cancer for individuals independent of 
smoking history. Additionally, there are some data 
showing that family history increases the individual risk 
for bladder cancer.
The most common presenting symptom of bladder 
cancer is painless intermittent gross hematuria. However, 
in locally advanced or metastatic disease, patients can be 
symptomatic with irritative or obstructive voiding symp­
toms, pain, and constitutional symptoms. A diagnosis of 
bladder cancer typically involves an office-based cystos­
copy. Urothelial abnormalities identified on cystoscopy 
require either an endoscopic biopsy or a transurethral 
resection of the abnormality for a pathologic diagnosis. 
Tumor size along with grade and depth of tumor invasion 
provide important prognostic and staging information and 
guide treatment planning.
For noninvasive disease (not invading the muscle), 
patients may then be considered for intravesical chemo­
therapy or immunotherapy with bacillus Calmette-Guerin. 
For invasive disease, patients may then be considered for 
either radical cystectomy with neoadjuvant or adjuvant 
therapy (with cisplatin-based chemotherapy) or trimodal 
therapy with maximal endoscopic resection followed by 
concurrent chemotherapy and radiotherapy.
Hematuria Follow-Up
Patients who have a negative malignancy that is a risk- 
stratified based evaluation and have negative urinalysis in 
a follow-up evaluation do not need further urologic 
evaluation. Many clinicians find it challenging to deter­
mine the need for repeat evaluation in patients who have 
persistent hematuria but a negative initial urologic evalu­
ation. The majority will not have an etiology identified in 
follow-up evaluations. A recent retrospective study found 
that of 637 patients with microscopic hematuria and a 
negative work-up (161 [25%] of whom had repeat 
cystoscopy and 317 [50%] of whom had repeat upper tract 
imaging at median 39 months), 2 (1.2%) had a new 
bladder malignancy identified and 4 (1.3%) had a new 
renal mass identified.

Kidney stones are often diagnosed or confirmed by 
imaging that is obtained in the setting of hematuria. Other 
clinical indications of kidney stones include flank pain or a 
previous history of kidney stones. Imaging modality 
choices to evaluate kidney stones include CT, ultrasound, 
and X-ray (kidney, ureter and bladder: KUB), and each has 
variable sensitivity and specificity. Noncontrast CT is the 
study of choice for imaging as part of the initial evaluation 
of patients who have acute onset of flank pain or suspicion 
of kidney stones. This is because the sensitivity and spec­
ificity of CT is much higher than other imaging modalities. 
Sensitivity of CT is reported to be as high as 0.95 and 
specificity as 0.97. However, in routine monitoring of 
stone burden, it is common to obtain ultrasound and KUB 
in order to reduce radiation exposure.
Kidney Stone Epidemiology
Kidney stones are common, and their prevalence has been 
rising—over 10% of individuals in the United States will 
get a kidney stone in their lifetime. Of patients presenting 
with hematuria, one retrospective review found that over 
11% have a kidney stone as the etiology of their micro­
scopic hematuria. The classic presentation of a kidney 
stone is flank pain and hematuria. The majority, but not

Risk Factors and Pathophysiology of Kidney 
Stones
There are many important clinical risk factors for kidney 
stones. These risk factors are commonly related to under­
lying genetics, systemic medical conditions, medications, 
and/or environmental or dietary exposures. For example, 
family history, diabetes, certain bowel diseases, and a low- 
calcium diet are all strongly associated with a higher risk of 
kidney stones. These clinical risk factors change the urine 
composition such that they increase the supersaturation of 
the urine with respect to the stone material (ie, calcium 
oxalate, calcium phosphate, or uric acid). By increasing the 
supersaturation, these factors then increase the risk of new 
stone formation or growth in the urinary space. A low- 
calcium diet, for example, increases urinary oxalate 
excretion and subsequently the calcium oxalate supersat­
uration and kidney stone risk.
Once a kidney stone forms it can cause both gross and 
microscopic hematuria. The hematuria from kidney stones 
likely results from damage to the mucosa of the upper or 
lower urinary tract from mechanical irritation, inflamma­
tion, and/or blockage.
Acute Management of Kidney Stones
The first consideration for acute management of a kidney 
stone passage event is assessing urgency of a urological 
consultation. The presence of signs including urinary tract 
infection (UTI), fever, acute kidney injury, uncontrollable 
pain, or obstruction on imaging requires an urgent uro­
logic consultation and consideration for urgent urologic 
intervention.
The majority of patients will not have these symptoms 
and can be managed conservatively in the outpatient 
setting. Patients may benefit from referral to urology for 
nonurgent urologic management in the outpatient setting. 
The 2 principles of conservative management are pain 
control and monitoring for stone passage.
Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory agents (NSAIDs) remain 
the foundation of management of kidney stone–related 
pain. A 2025 Cochrane systematic review that included 
3,593 patients from 29 different trials found that NSAIDs 
were effective at reducing pain compared with placebo. 
Additionally, in a 2018 systematic review that included 
4,887 patients and 36 trials, NSAIDs were slightly superior 
to opioids in pain reduction at 30 minutes and had an 
improved side-effect profile with less vomiting compared 
with patients treated with opioids. However, NSAID use 
should be limited in patients with reduced estimated 
glomerular filtration rate at baseline and are not recom­
mended for patients with acute kidney injury.
The likelihood of stone passage without a urologic 
intervention is heavily dependent on stone size. Stones 
under 5 mm are likely to pass on their own without 
pharmacologic therapy. Stones greater than 5 mm may 
require intervention to pass about 50% of the time. For 
stones between 5 mm and 1 cm, the likelihood of 
requiring intervention increases, and medical expulsive 
therapy (MET) is commonly prescribed. Because the pa­
tient in this case has a 6-mm stone, MET and pain control 
is an appropriate acute management strategy, so the 
answer to Question 3 is (b).
MET typically includes α-blocker medications, such as 
tamsulosin. Although the usefulness of MET has been 
debated, meta-analyses and a Cochrane review have shown 
that 4 weeks of MET with an α-blocker may help sponta­
neous passage, and the AUA guidelines support the use of 
MET with α-blockers for uncomplicated ureteral stones ≤10 
mm. Stones larger than 1 cm are unlikely to pass sponta­
neously or with MET and will likely require urologic 
intervention. For all stone sizes, the time to stone passage 
can vary, with some stones taking several weeks to pass.
When following a patient for spontaneous passage or 
on MET, it is important to consider imaging to confirm 
that the patient has passed the stone. Some patients will 
have asymptomatic obstruction, and the clinical symptoms 
(eg, cessation of pain) may not accurately predict the 
resolution of hydronephrosis or stone passage.
Surgical intervention is warranted for patients who 
have prolonged obstruction (4-6 weeks), have large 
volume stone disease, or are unable to tolerate conser­
vative management (ongoing pain, nausea, vomiting, 
kidney dysfunction). The common surgical intervention 
options for stone disease include shockwave lithotripsy, 
ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy, and percutaneous 
nephrolithotomy.
The decision on the specific intervention modality is 
based on stone factors such as the stone size, type, loca­
tion, and density; patient factors such as kidney function, 
kidney anatomy, and comorbidities; and the goals of 
treatment. In general, ureteroscopy and shockwave litho­
tripsy are effective treatments for smaller stones whereas a 
percutaneous approach is reserved for larger volume stones 
(>2 cm) or stones that fail treatment with less invasive

Preventative Strategies for Kidney Stones
Kidney stone prevention is also a crucial part of manage­
ment for patients who form kidney stones. Referral to 
nephrology for a metabolic work-up is recommended for 
recurrent kidney stone patients and for patients who have a 
solitary kidney; and it also should be considered for other 
groups who are at high risk for recurrence, such as those 
with cystinuria or uric acid stones.
The initial metabolic work-up for recurrent kidney 
stone patients includes a basic metabolic panel with serum 
calcium, urinalysis, 24-hour urine collections, and a stone 
composition analysis (if available), so the answer to 
Question 4 is (d). Given the day-to-day variation in dietary 
patterns, it is common to obtain two 24-hour urine col­
lections during the initial work-up. The 24-hour urine 
collections are critical for preventative therapy strategies 
because they provide clinical data to guide targeted pre­
vention strategies based on the individual’s specific urinary 
risk factors, such as hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria, or 
hypocitraturia. Additional work-up, including parathyroid 
hormone levels and genetic testing is recommended if 
indicated after initial testing.
Individualized Strategies for Kidney Stone 
Prevention
A prevention plan that is individualized to a patient’s 24- 
hour urine collection is associated with improved kidney 
stone outcomes with lower rates of recurrence and pro­
cedures. The overall goal is to reduce the urine supersat­
uration of the stone type (eg, calcium oxalate, calcium 
phosphate, or uric acid) as this is expected to reduce the 
risk of stone formation. To do so, one should identify 
which modifiable parameters in the 24-hour urine are 
contributing to a high supersaturation. For example, if 
urine calcium levels are high, then one should consider 
high sodium or protein intake as potential contributors to 
the high urine calcium by examining the 24-hour urine 
sodium and protein catabolic rate. The dietary advice 
should prioritize the factor(s) contributing to high urine 
calcium for that individual. This may include reducing 
dietary sodium (if the sodium intake is greater than 2,300 
mg/day) or reducing protein intake (if the protein intake 
is above moderate). Once dietary modifications are opti­
mized, pharmacologic therapies should also be considered.
After initiating a preventative strategy, a single repeat 
24-hour urine collection can evaluate the success of the 
strategy and guide the ongoing preventative strategy. A 
similar approach is recommended for other urinary ab­
normalities associated with increased supersaturation and 
kidney stone risk, such as high urine oxalate, low urine 
citrate, and low urine pH.

Diagnosis of Glomerular Hematuria
Glomerular etiologies of hematuria are more likely when 
dysmorphic RBCs or RBC casts, proteinuria, or impairment 
in kidney function are also present. If a patient with 
glomerular bleeding has gross hematuria, a brown, cola- 
colored urine may be present. In this setting, a kidney 
biopsy may be indicated.
During an initial work-up for hematuria, as a part of the 
history and physical examination one should screen for 
specific features that are suggestive of glomerular causes of 
the hematuria. For example, a family history of hematuria 
could be suggestive of thin basement membrane or Alport 
syndrome. A family history of kidney failure of unclear 
etiology may be suggestive of Alport syndrome. A personal 
or family history of sensorineural hearing loss may be 
consistent with Alport syndrome. A history of acute upper 
respiratory infection preceding onset of hematuria may be 
consistent with IgA nephropathy.
The initial serologic work-up should include an esti­
mate of kidney function. Complement levels and the 
antinuclear antibody should be considered to complete the 
evaluation for the less common glomerular etiologies of 
hematuria, C3 glomerulopathy, and lupus nephritis. 
Additionally, depending on the clinical situation, one can 
consider obtaining double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil 
cytoplasmic antibodies, and antiglomerular basement 
membrane antibody.
To establish a diagnosis, a kidney biopsy may be indi­
cated. Clinicians should participate in shared decision 
making with the patient about the risks and benefits of a 
kidney biopsy, but the biopsy may be indicated in settings 
of positive serologies, proteinuria, hypertension, reduced 
kidney function, or during evaluation for living donor 
transplantation. Persistent unexplained microscopic he­
maturia may also be an indication for kidney biopsy if a 
definitive diagnosis is desired, once urologic causes have 
been excluded. Finally, genetic testing may be an impor­
tant part of the diagnosis for conditions such as Alport 
syndrome.
IgA Nephropathy
IgA nephropathy is the most common cause of primary 
glomerular disease worldwide, and it is caused by deposits 
of IgA in the glomerular mesangium. A study in the 
People’s Republic of China found that among 13,519 
kidney biopsies, over 45% were IgA nephropathy. A 
separate study of biopsy diagnoses found that over 22% of 
diagnoses from Europe and 11% from North America were 
IgA nephropathy.
The clinical features of IgA nephropathy can vary 
because there is a large degree of clinical and pathological 
heterogeneity. Children and young adults in North 
America commonly present with macroscopic hematuria 
during another illness (ie, respiratory or gastrointestinal) 
whereas adults typically present with microscopic hema­
turia, with or without proteinuria, hypertension, or 
reduced kidney function. IgA nephropathy is also 
commonly associated with other conditions, such as 
chronic liver disease, autoimmune conditions, and viral 
infections (eg, hepatitis or HIV).
The diagnosis of IgA nephropathy requires a kidney 
biopsy because there are no validated serum or urine 
biomarkers for IgA nephropathy. The Kidney Disease 
Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2025 Clinical 
Practice Guidelines on the management of IgA nephropa­
thy recommend considering kidney biopsy for patients 
where IgA nephropathy is possible and urine protein is at 
least 0.5 g/day. Further, once the diagnosis of IgA is 
established, it is recommended to screen for secondary 
causes such as liver disease.
Alport Syndrome
Alport syndrome is a genetic disease caused by variants in 
α-chains of type IV collagen of the basement membranes 
of the kidney, eye, and cochlea. In the United States, the 
incidence of Alport syndrome is estimated at about 3% of 
children and 0.2% of adults with end-stage kidney disease. 
Genetic transmission of Alport syndrome is most often X- 
linked with variants of the COL4A5 gene, but it can also be 
autosomal recessive or autosomal dominant with variants 
of either the COL4A3 or COL4A4 genes. Additionally, dis­
ease penetration varies based on environmental and other 
genetic factors.
The clinical features of Alport syndrome reflect the 
distribution of affected type IV collagen in the basement 
membranes in the kidney, eye, and cochlea. These gene 
variants lead to heterogeneous abnormalities, including 
glomerular disease (microscopic hematuria, progressive 
kidney disease, and end-stage kidney disease), ocular ab­
normalities, and sensorineural deafness.
Alport syndrome commonly presents with persistent 
asymptomatic microscopic hematuria in childhood. Men 
presenting with classic X-linked disease will have more 
advanced disease while patients presenting with autosomal 
dominant disease or women with X-linked disease may 
have a variable clinical presentation and course.
For patients who have a positive family history of Alport 
syndrome or who have physical examination features 
consistent with Alport syndrome (ie, sensorineural hearing 
loss or ocular findings), the molecular genetic testing can 
confirm the diagnosis. For this woman with microscopic 
hematuria and a family history of hearing loss, a genetics 
panel can confirm the diagnosis, so the correct answer is 
(b). Biopsy of skin or kidney tissue can also lead to 
diagnosis. This information can also be used for genetic

Thin Basement Membrane Nephropathy
Thin basement membrane nephropathy (TBMN) is a 
common condition that has a strong genetic component 
and is characterized by diffuse thinning of the glomerular 
basement membrane. It has some similarity to Alport 
syndrome: heterogenous variants to COL4A3 and COL4A4 
have been identified in some, but not all, patients and 
families with TBMN. Although it was previously consid­
ered a benign condition, it has been associated with focal 
segmental glomerulosclerosis.
The main clinical feature of TBMN is asymptomatic 
microscopic hematuria. Patients typically present without 
other abnormalities though some may present with 
microscopic hematuria and mild to moderate proteinuria 
or hypertension. A kidney biopsy with electron micro­
scopy will confirm the diagnosis, but not all patients 
require a biopsy. The diagnosis can often be based on the 
benign clinical presentation (ie, isolated hematuria 
without proteinuria or reduced kidney function) in the 
setting of a family history of hematuria (without kidney 
failure). If a patient with a TBMN diagnosis develops new 
or worsening clinical features, such as worsening pro­
teinuria, it is important to consider other conditions such 
as Alport syndrome or IgA nephropathy. It is possible that 
Alport syndrome may be initially diagnosed as TBMN if 
the biopsy is performed early.
Treatment
The treatment for glomerular causes of hematuria depends 
on the final diagnosis.
For IgA nephropathy, the KDIGO guideline for IgA 
nephropathy management was recently updated. It is rec­
ommended to assess the risk of progression using histo­
logic findings from the biopsy, the estimated glomerular 
filtration rate, and the degree of proteinuria. Based on the 
risk of progression and overall treatment goals, the treat­
ment strategies include general supportive care with or 
without immunosuppressive therapy. General supportive 
care includes renin-angiotensin system (RAS) blockade, 
blood pressure control, lifestyle modifications (eg, low 
sodium diet, smoking cessation), and consideration of 
additional therapies to target both proteinuria and the 
immune pathogenesis of the disease.
For Alport syndrome, clinical practice guidelines were 
also recently updated. In this update, RAS blockade re­
mains the standard of care. RAS blockade should be initi­
ated at diagnosis for both men with X-linked and for 
individuals with autosomal recessive Alport syndrome. 
Women with X-linked and all individuals with autosomal 
dominant Alport syndrome are recommended to have RAS 
blockade initiated at the onset of albuminuria. RAS 
blockade is standard because studies have found that RAS 
blockade delays end-stage kidney disease and increases the 
patient’s life expectancy compared with no treatment. 
Other therapies, such as cyclosporine, have shown 
inconsistent data and are not recommended.
Patients with Alport syndrome who undergo kidney 
transplantation have excellent outcomes and do not have 
disease recurrence. However, it is recommended to follow 
them closely, particularly in the first year after transplant, 
and to have a low threshold for allograft biopsy because 
about 3% of men can acquire posttransplant anti­
glomerular basement membrane disease.

Pathogenesis of Hematuria in Sickle Cell Trait

multiple causes. Papillary necrosis or infarct is a common

renal medullary carcinoma, particularly when there is no 
other clear explanation for the hematuria. Renal medullary 
carcinoma is an aggressive non-clear cell renal cancer that 
is associated with sickle cell trait and continues to have 
poor survival outcomes, despite new therapies.
Diagnosis
When evaluating a patient with sickle cell trait and he­
maturia, a urinalysis with microscopy and culture should 
be included in the diagnostic work-up. Given the high risk 
of urothelial malignancy due to the risk of renal medullary 
carcinoma, these individuals should also be considered for 
upper urinary tract imaging. There are no data to guide the 
optimal choice of imaging, but most experts recommend 
obtaining CT imaging of the abdomen and pelvis.
Treatment
Management of hematuria from a UTI is similar to man­
agement of a UTI in a patient who does not have sickle cell 
trait. Management of papillary necrosis is supportive. For 
the rare patient with renal medullary carcinoma, the cur­
rent standard of care therapies include nephrectomy and 
platinum-based chemotherapy.

Conclusion
Hematuria is a common and clinically significant finding 
across primary care, nephrology, and urology practices. 
Prompt and accurate diagnosis is essential to distinguish 
between benign and serious underlying causes, including 
malignancy, glomerular disease, nephrolithiasis, and more 
rare genetic syndromes. The current AUA guidelines 
advocate a risk-stratified approach to evaluation of hema­
turia, ensuring that high-risk patients receive comprehen­
sive diagnostic evaluation in a timely manner while 
minimizing unnecessary procedures in low-risk individuals.
