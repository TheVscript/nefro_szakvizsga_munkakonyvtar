Hemodialysis Vascular Access: Core
Curriculum 2025
Charmaine E. Lok, Theodore Yuo, and Timmy Lee
The majority of patients with kidney failure requiring replacement therapy will need the support of
hemodialysis during their journey with kidney failure. A reliable functioning vascular access is required
to provide hemodialysis. This Core Curriculum reviews the major forms of vascular access (arterio-
venous ﬁstula, arteriovenous graft, and central venous catheter) as well as the planning, preparation,
creation, use, and maintenance of vascular access, requiring a P-L-A-N (Patient ESKD Life-Plan ﬁrst
then Access Needs) for each patient. The end-stage kidney disease Patient Life-Plan focuses on a
strategy for kidney replacement modalities, while the Access Needs are the corresponding dialysis
access(es) and management plans. The Access Needs include a vessel preservation plan, creation
plan, contingency (complications) plan, and access succession plan. Stenosis and thrombosis are
common problems with arteriovenous accesses, and dysfunction and infection are common problems
with central venous catheters. Underrecognized and underreported but potentially life-threatening
situations include arteriovenous access rupture and high-output cardiac failure. Effective manage-
ment of these and other vascular access problems requires a coordinated multidisciplinary effort that is
patient centered while preserving vascular access.

Access Needs: Choosing AV Access
Once her ESKD Life-Plan has been determined, the pa-
tient in Case 1 will establish her access plan (ie, Access
Needs). The creation plan is composed of many ele-
ments that help determine her “lifeline”: the type of
vascular access to be created, who creates it, how it is
created, and when and where it is created. However, the
ability to have vascular access depends on the patient
having viable vessels to create AV access, so protecting
and preserving vessels in a patient with CKD is essential,
so the answer is (d). An AVF, created by an anastomosis
between a native artery and a vein, is the recommended
vascular access for patients requiring HD due to its
substantially lower rates of thrombosis, infection, and
health care–related expenditures compared with AVG
and CVC, if the AVF successfully matures for dialysis use.
However, many AVFs do not mature successfully to
support dialysis and will need interventions to assist
with maturation. Studies have demonstrated that AVFs
that need multiple interventions before use (eg, recur-
rent angioplasties to correct stenosis, ligations for
collateral veins to divert ﬂow from a maturing AVF, etc)
have a lower cumulative patency (ie, AV access patency
from creation to abandonment) than AVFs that do not
need such interventions. For this reason, choosing a
vascular access type that can be used with minimal in-
terventions and that concurrently preserves vessels for
future needed access is critically important.

Access Needs: Indications for Catheter Use
Case 2: A 71-year-old retired shop owner with long-standing
uncontrolled diabetes, hypertension, peripheral vascular dis-
ease, CKD, and gout has presented with malaise, anorexia,
fever, and cough. Laboratory testing reveals leukocytosis and
a creatinine level of 548 μmol/L (6.2 mg/dL). His chest X-ray
identiﬁes a right lower lobe pneumonia, and a kidney ultra-
sound demonstrates small echogenic kidneys. Despite
treatment of the pneumonia, his kidney function worsens,
and he becomes oliguric. What are the immediate and
longer-term vascular access options for this patient?
In the United States, approximately 80% of patients
initiate HD with a CVC. In situations of emergent or
urgent need for dialysis, as in this case, a CVC (non-
tunneled or tunneled, depending on the situation) for
temporary use is appropriate to manage intoxications,
uremic symptoms, or volume overload refractory to
medical management. Tunneled CVC inserted into the
internal jugular vein is the most commonly placed for HD
and should be inserted with imaging guidance to ensure
the CVC tip resides in the mid right atrium. Nontunneled
“temporary” CVC should be inserted under ultrasound
guidance and should only be in place for 2 weeks to
reduce the risk of catheter-related infection. There are
valid circumstances for long-term use of tunneled CVC
(Box 1); therefore, the terminology to designate AV ac-
cess (AVF or AVG) as “permanent access” should not be
used because CVC can also be used as permanent vascular
access in select valid situations.
CVC Complications
Poor Flows. The key complications with CVC use
include CVC dysfunction, CVC-related infections, and
central venous stenosis (discussed later). Both CVC
dysfunction and infection can occur early or late from the
time of insertion, and these separate complications may or
may not be related. CVC dysfunction can occur due to
mechanical factors, such as CVC malpositioning or kinks,
and from intraluminal (thrombosis) and extraluminal
(ﬁbrin sheath coating) factors.
Treatment is typically with installation of a thrombo-
lytic agent (eg, tissue plasminogen activator) using dwell
or push techniques. If this is unsuccessful in restoration of
ﬂow, a central venogram may be performed to identify a
ﬁbrin sheath, which should be disrupted with balloon
angioplasty; typically, a new CVC is required, which is
usually inserted by guidewire exchange to preserve sites.
Prevention is based on anticoagulant interdialytic locking
(typically heparin or low-concentration 4% trisodium
citrate) and use of thrombolytic locks in situations of
recurrent CVC dysfunction.
Infections. CVC-related infections include exit site, tun-
nel, and CVC-related bloodstream infections (CRBSI), which
are the most dangerous and can progress to life-threatening
sepsis. The pathophysiology of CRBSI initiates from an
extraluminal or intraluminal source. Typically, extraluminal
sources of infection occur early on after CVC insertion (eg,
bacterial contamination of a nonhealed exit site or tunnel),
and the risk of intraluminal sources of bacteria persists as long
as the CVC is in place. Intraluminal sources occur with CVC
manipulation by health providers or home dialysis patients
managing their own CVC: bacterial organisms from the
external environment are transferred to the CVC lumen after
breaches in aseptic no-touch or sterile techniques.

CRBSI should be suspected in any patient with a HD
CVC and signs and/or symptoms of a bacteremia after
excluding alternative sources of infection. The signs and/
or symptoms of CRBSI include fever, chills, new onset
and unexplained fatigue, malaise, or altered level of
consciousness. Hypotension and/or rigors within 15-20
minutes of initiating dialysis is consistent with endotoxin
release from disruption of bacteria from the CVC surface
caused by the increased blood ﬂow through the CVC, and
this should raise suspicion of a CRBSI, particularly when
there is no clinical evidence for an alternate source. A
thorough history and physical examination will help
exclude alternative sources and may include appropriate
cultures (urine, sputum, wound, or drainage collec-
tions), chest X-ray, or electrocardiogram.
Two sets of blood cultures should be obtained from the
catheter (eg, hubs) and from the dialysis bloodline circuit or
peripheral vein, so answer (a) is correct. In practical terms,
the dialysis bloodline circuit is often preferred as the pe-
ripheral sample source because peripheral vein cultures are
often challenging to obtain, may damage veins for future
vascular access, and are more easily contaminated than the
dialysis circuit. Both the dialysis bloodline circuit and pe-
ripheral vein are considered peripheral sources.
The diagnosis of CRBSI requires concurrent blood cul-
tures positive for the same organism from 2 sites (ideally
both catheter source and peripheral source) in the absence
of an alternative source of infection. If there is apparent
infection at the exit site or tunnel (eg, redness, pain, or
discharge) then cultures should be taken from these sites as
corroborative diagnostic data.

Management of CVC-related Infection
The 2 components of treatment include management of
the patient and management of the CVC (Fig 2). Once CVC
infection is suspected, appropriate cultures of the exit site,
tunnel, and/or blood should be obtained before initiating
empiric antibiotics in order to properly identify the or-
ganism and determine antibiotic susceptibility. The choice
of empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics depends on local
dialysis facility policies and organism antibiotic sensitiv-
ities. Further reﬁnement should occur with the culture
sensitivity results to ensure effective treatment and to avoid
antibiotic resistance, so the answer is (c). A hemody-
namically unstable patient should be ﬂuid resuscitated and
transported to the hospital or emergency department for
further care. If the unstable patient needs urgent dialysis,
the CVC can be used once before its removal.
If stable, the patient can be managed in the outpatient
setting. Management of the CVC depends on the type of
CVC infection, the urgency for dialysis, the patient’s
symptoms and hemodynamic stability, the infecting or-
ganism, the infection history and risk factors, and the
feasibility of CVC replacement. In general, there are 4
options for CVC management of CRBSI: (1) salvage with
antibiotic lock; (2) CVC exchange over guidewire to
preserve sites so long as the exit site and tunnel are not
infected; (3) CVC removal with a new insertion at a
separate site at the same time; or (4) CVC removal with a
new insertion at a separate site after a CVC free period.
This requires the temporary use of a nontunneled CVC to
provide interim dialysis if needed.
If the infected CVC represents the last feasible vascular
access option, then the patient should continue with the
CVC with treatment by concurrent antibiotic lock and
systemic antibiotics. If the CVC can be removed, it should
be replaced by guidewire exchange to limit vessel damage
and preserve vascular access options. If there is an exit site
or tunnel infection, if the symptoms do not resolve after
48-72 hours of treatment with systemic antibiotics, or if
the infecting organism is particularly virulent (eg, Staphy-
lococcus aureus or fungal), then CVC removal with reinsertion
of a temporary nontunneled CVC at a new site is indicated.
Ideally, repeat blood cultures should be negative before
insertion of a new tunneled CVC. However, sometimes it is
only feasible to have reinsertion at a new site at the time of
removal. In such cases, evaluation for the presence of ﬁbrin
sheath (which is likely to be infected) and its disruption can
remove the source of ongoing or future recurrent infection.
The duration of treatment depends on the infecting
organism, with most requiring 2-3 weeks of systemic
antibiotics. S aureus, fungal, and some Gram-negative or-
ganisms or polymicrobial infections require a minimum of
4 weeks or longer if there is associated metastatic infection
(cardiac, lung, brain or bone). Thus, evaluation for
metastasis with aggressive organisms, including two-

Prevention of CVC-related Infection
A primary target of the new KDOQI guidelines for
vascular access is to have a CRBSI rate of <1.5/1,000
CVC days. The best prophylaxis of CRBSI is not to have
a CVC; however, this is often not feasible. CVC use
should be limited to the shortest duration possible.
Because CRBSI is associated with signiﬁcant morbidity
and mortality, proper prophylaxis against CRBSI is crit-
ical. To effectively implement a prophylactic strategy, 2
important points should be considered: (1) the baseline
CRSBI rate in the facility as a benchmark to determine
whether the prophylactic strategies are effective and (2)
targeting the prophylactic strategy based on the patho-
physiology of the CRSBI.
It should be a given that general universal precautions are
in place and that health care providers and patients are
aware of best practices for HD catheter care, including
excellent hand hygiene. Speciﬁc strategies to prevent CRBSI
target the extraluminal route by creating barriers to or-
ganism entry and disinfecting at the exit site, and those
targeting the intraluminal route aim to “sterilize” the lumen
or disrupt the catheter bioﬁlm once organisms have entered.

AV Access Complications
Maturation Failure, Stenosis, and Thrombosis
AV access maturation failure is deﬁned as an AV access
that, despite radiologic or surgical intervention (ie,
endovascular or open procedural management), cannot
be used successfully for dialysis by 6 months after its
creation. Rates of AV access maturation failure have
been reported to be 20%-60% in AVF and 5%-20% in
AVG.
AVF maturation failure and AVG dysfunction are most
commonly
characterized on
angiography
by
venous
stenosis. In AVF, the venous stenosis frequently occurs at
the juxta-anastomotic region, the region of the AVF
adjacent to the vein-artery anastomosis, but stenoses can
occur at cannulation segments and in the outﬂow of the
AVF. In AVG, a stenosis occurs most commonly at the
vein-graft anastomosis. In AVF maturation failure, the
most common etiology of venous stenosis is secondary
to
ﬂow-limiting
intimal
hyperplasia
and
inadequate
outward remodeling (Fig 3). In AVG failure, the most
common etiology of venous stenosis is secondary to
aggressive intimal hyperplasia formation, characterized
by the presence of myoﬁbroblasts and an abundance of
extracellular
matrix
components
within
the
intima,
leading
to
AVG
thrombosis
(Fig
3).
Angiogenesis
(neovascularization) within the adventitia layer and a
macrophage layer lining the perigraft region are also
present in AVGs, which is another major difference from
the venous stenosis seen in AVF failure.
Currently, there are few effective therapies to prevent
venous stenosis in AVF and AVG. Aspirin-dipyridamole
and ﬁsh oil may provide primary patency beneﬁt and
reduce the need for interventions to maintain patency for
AVGs. There are no other medications—including statins,
warfarin, or other systemic agents—that have proven
beneﬁcial to avoid AV access dysfunction.

AV Access Monitoring and Surveillance
AV access (AVF or AVG) ﬂow-related dysfunction, most
commonly secondary to stenosis and thrombosis, occurs
frequently during the lifetime of an AV access. The ratio-
nale for monitoring and supplementary surveillance is that
detection of a vascular access abnormality (ie, stenosis) in
AVF and AVG can lead to a pre-emptive intervention to
correct a stenosis and prevent a vascular access thrombosis
from occurring, and improve overall vascular access
patency and function.
One important strategy of routine clinical monitoring is
routine physical examination of the vascular access
(Table 3). The main principles of physical examination
include inspection, palpation, and auscultation of the
vascular access to detect for signs of stenosis and patho-
logic abnormalities within the vascular access. Monitoring
by physical
examination can
also be
supplemented
assessing trends in monthly dialysis adequacy studies (ie,
reductions in Kt/V or urea reduction ratio without other
cause), increasing venous pressures, and other clinical
ﬁndings such as aspiration of blood clots, cannulation is-
sues when previously not an issue, and prolonged bleeding
after dialysis needle withdrawal (Box 2).
Surveillance utilizes specialized equipment and/or op-
erators to detect stenosis. Several techniques measure AV
access ﬂow (Qa) including duplex ultrasound and ultra-
sound dilution. Dynamic and static venous pressure are
also additional techniques for surveillance.
Clinical trials in AVG comparing routine clinical moni-
toring versus surveillance have largely demonstrated no dif-
ference in AVG survival with surveillance as compared with
clinical monitoring. One study showed beneﬁt of surveillance
in AVG. In AVFs, there are few clinical trials studying
monitoring versus surveillance. In these studies, surveillance
in addition to clinical monitoring appears to increase the
detection of stenosis but also increases the rate of in-
terventions without improving patency. A recent randomized
controlled trial (RCT) found no increase in interventions but
also no difference in rate of or time to ﬁrst thrombotic event
of AVF and AVG. A systematic review and meta-analysis of
RCTs assessed active AV access surveillance (by measuring
Doppler ﬂow or venous pressure) and pre-emptive correc-
tion of a newly identiﬁed stenosis versus routine clinical
monitoring and deferred salvage, or pre-emptive correction
of a known stenosis versus deferred salvage. In this meta-
analysis, pre-emptive stenosis intervention for a functional
AV access does not improve AV access patency. The main
ﬁndings were that pre-emptive correction of an AV access
stenosis may reduce the risk for thrombosis but not the risk
for AV access loss. The treatment effects were larger in AVF as
compared to the AVG group for both risk for AVF access loss
and risk for thrombosis.
In this case, the clinical indicators of recurrent prolonged
bleeding and concurrent elevated venous pressures should
prompt conﬁrmatory evaluation regardless of the Kt/V, so
the answer is (c). Surveillance measures can be done for
supplementary information but should not delay treatment.

Although the current and preferred vascular access re-
mains the traditional surgical upper extremity AV access, it
may be difﬁcult or prohibited by bilateral central vein
occlusions. Central venous occlusion is an important later
complication of CVC use that must always be considered
when choosing to insert a CVC; this highlights the
importance of using CVC exchange over a guidewire at the
same site, whenever possible, to preserve central veins
from damage and occlusion. There have been several
recent novel innovations in devices and technology for HD
vascular access care, including those that consider such
challenges when bilateral central venous occlusion occurs.
These innovations can be divided into several categories:
(1) new vascular access creation, (2) maintenance of
vascular access patency, and (3) future innovations in the
pipeline. The innovations for new vascular access creation
(AVF and AVG) include (1) endovascular AVF creation,
(2) bioengineered grafts, (3) early cannulation grafts, and
(4) hybrid devices.
In this speciﬁc case presented, several devices may be
potentially applicable for this patient’s central vein occlu-
sions. Of note, complete central venous occlusion pro-
hibits successful AV access creation and use, and until
recently could not be bypassed. In 2020, the US Food and
Drug Administration (FDA) approved a new device
referred to as “Surfacer,”, which allows for insertion of a
dialysis catheter in the right supraclavicular region of pa-
tients with right-sided central vein occlusion, answer (d),
by obtaining vascular access “inside to out”. In short,
procedurally, under ﬂuoroscopy after obtaining access to
the right femoral vein, a stiff metal sheath is passed su-
periorly into the right atrium, and a new tract is created
through the soft tissues in the chest, bypassing the
occluded thoracic central vein segment on the right side of
the body. A central catheter (HD CVC or CVC component
of a HERO graft) can then be placed by Seldinger tech-
nique over the wire in the tract created. Careful patient
selection and an experienced operator are critical because
the device is not steerable; hemorrhagic complications
have occurred and can be fatal. In a multicenter study
performed in Europe, this novel device demonstrated a
97% success rate with achieving central venous access and
dialysis catheter placement.
Another late-stage vascular access option for patients
receiving HD with limited venous anatomy is the Hemo-
dialysis Reliable Outﬂow (HERO) device. This device is a
hybrid-graft catheter (polytetraﬂuorethylene [PTFE] graft
connected to a silicone catheter venous outﬂow compo-
nent) that is implanted subcutaneously. The device can be
inserted through stenotic but patent central vein segments
in patients with suboptimal peripheral veins and terminates
in the right atrium. Studies have demonstrated the technical
feasibility of placement of the HERO device and comparable
results in terms of patency to standard PTFE grafts; when
compared with dialysis catheters, HERO had better patency,
fewer interventions, and lower infection rates.

AV Access Corrective Interventions to Maintain
Patency
Although future innovative AV access solutions are prom-
ising, recurrence of stenosis, especially juxta-anastomotic
stenosis, currently remains the most common cause of AV
access dysfunction. For years, the standard treatment of AV
access dysfunction has been standard balloon angioplasty
(percutaneous transluminal angioplasty [PTA]), which has
been shown to induce endothelial injury leading to intimal
hyperplasia. New technology such as drug-coated balloons
(DCB) combined with standard PTA and endovascular stent
grafts has been shown in RCTs to prolong patency. DCBs
deliver an antiproliferative therapy (eg, paclitaxel or siroli-
mus) to prevent intimal hyperplasia after PTA. A recent
landmark clinical trial demonstrated that angioplasty with
DCB was superior to standard PTA when evaluating the
primary outcome of treatment of stenotic lesions in
dysfunctional AVF during the 6 months after the procedure.
Subsequent RCTs and meta-analysis have had conﬂicting
results, suggesting that longer term follow-up and experi-
ence are required to determine the role of DCBs in vascular
access management.
Stent grafts are a self-expanding vascular prothesis
composed of PTFE encapsulating a nitinol stent scaffold.
Stent grafts have been shown to improve the primary
patency of the target lesion of the vascular access as
compared with standard PTA. An early large multicenter
clinical trial in 190 patients with dysfunctional AVG with
stenosis at the venous anastomosis demonstrated that stent-
grafts, as compared with standard PTA, improved patency
at 6 months with fewer repeat interventions. Subsequent
meta-analyses supported this ﬁnding and extended its
beneﬁt to longer durations.
Promising new innovations in the pipeline may posi-
tively impact AV access outcomes. For example, the VasQ
(Laminate Medical Technologies) device manipulates the
anastomotic angle and geometry to reduce vein wall ten-
sion and control ﬂow patterns. Another technology is
perivascular delivery of a drug such as nitric oxide that can
promote outward remodeling and inhibit intimal hyper-
plasia. Other technologies, such as those to facilitate the
monitoring of AV access, are being evaluated.

Vascular Access Hemorrhage
Fatal vascular access hemorrhage (FVAH) is a rare but
dramatic and catastrophic complication of AV access.
Successful management and prevention are of highest
concern for both the patient and the dialysis and vascular
access care team. The risk factors for FVAH include AV
access-related complications such as difﬁculty with can-
nulation or poor ﬂow characteristics, in addition to the
development of concerning physical examination ﬁndings
(Table 4). In this case, use of a clotting material over the
cannulation site caused fragile skin to adhere to the ma-
terial and pull off part of the scab upon its removal, risking
bleeding. Furthermore, use of an area cannulation tech-
nique without adequate site rotation as opposed to the
preferred rope ladder technique has also been associated
with poor outcomes; the answer to Question 8 is thus (b).
Both AVF and AVG can be involved in FVAH; the relative
risk of one form of AV access over the other is unclear.
FAVH can occur with all types of HD and in all geographic
locations—in-center, satellite, and home HD. These events
frequently occur outside the dialysis unit, with profound
blood loss evident at the scene. This can lead to ﬁrst re-
sponders sometimes mistakenly concluding that a homi-
cide or a suicide has occurred.
The ﬁrst step to management is direct pressure over the
bleeding oriﬁce. This may be inadequate, and use of a
tourniquet can be lifesaving. Critically, the tourniquet must
be placed above the bleeding point, and it must be tight-
ened to above systolic blood pressure to stop the ﬂow
through the AV access; thus, the answer to Question 9 is (c).
Inadequate tension will be ineffective and may actually in-
crease the blood loss because any venous return is obliter-
ated while arterial ﬂow is still present. As such, it may be
challenging for a patient to ﬁnd an appropriate tourniquet,
be it a rope or belt, to exert the required force. Furthermore,
successful cessation of arterial ﬂow will lead to the affected
limb becoming ischemic and may lead to limb loss without
emergent assistance. It must be highlighted that AV access
rupture is a medical and surgical emergency that requires
immediate professional attention.
Until medical help is available, use of a carefully placed
bottle cap before full rupture has been described. It is
important to note that the hollow side is used over the
bleeding oriﬁce, and ﬁrm pressure is required to obtain
hemostasis, which can be uncomfortable for the patient
(Fig 4A-B). It is critical to underline that rapid surgical
evaluation is required: this is only a temporizing measure,
and the AV access will rapidly bleed again once the cap is
removed.
Prevention is key, so it is critical to recognize the signs of
imminent AVF rupture, such as necrotic scabs (Fig 4C-D;
Video 1) or suppurative puncture sites; thus, the answer to
Question 10 is (a). If these signs are present, it is important
to immediately refer the patient to surgery for evaluation.
AV accesses with aneurysms or pseudoaneurysms are at
particularly high risk of rupture when they are associated
with concerning physical examination ﬁndings. These are
outlined in the 2019 KDOQI guidelines (Table 4). Of note,
size by itself is not considered to be a risk factor, as non-
aneurysmal AV accesses can also rupture. Although a large
aneurysmal
AVF
(“megaﬁstula”)
without
concerning

of rupture, their inevitably high ﬂow rates may be associ-
ated with cardiac issues. Thus, both AV access and cardiac
status should be monitored carefully and referred for revi-
sion (ie, ﬂow-reduction surgery) or ligation as needed.

High Cardiac Output Heart Failure
The increased ﬂow associated with AV access creation
can signiﬁcantly inﬂuence cardiac hemodynamics and
lead to structural and functional cardiovascular abnor-
malities. For example, it can lead to high cardiac output
(CO) with subsequent heart failure and pulmonary hy-
pertension. In patients with KF requiring HD, approxi-
mately 25% of cases of high output heart failure can be
attributed to AV access. Due to the higher access ﬂows
(Qa) in the upper arm versus forearm AVF (ie, approx-
imately double Qa in the upper arm), high output car-
diac failure occurs more commonly with the higher ﬂow
upper arm AV access.
This is important to consider when developing the
patient’s ESKD Life-Plan and Access Plan. For example, in
an elderly patient who has limited life expectancy and is
at high risk of AVF failure due to the presence of poor
vasculature and heart failure, one might consider an
upper arm AVF because upper arm vessels are typically
larger than forearm vessels and an upper arm AVF might
have a higher likelihood of AVF maturation success. The
consideration of “preserving vessel real estate” is not as
critical because the patient has limited life expectancy, so
the priority is getting functional, “complication-free”
vascular access quickly. However, in this situation, the
patient has underlying heart failure, so the vascular ac-
cess implications must be carefully considered.
First, an AVF may not mature, even if created in the
upper arm. In order for an AVF to mature, not only
does it require good inﬂow (ie, not by a calciﬁed,
diseased, or damaged artery) and unobstructed, patent
venous outﬂow, it requires a good pump (cardiac
function) to provide the good inﬂow and pressure
required for AVF maturation. Further, an upper arm
AVF with high ﬂow (eg, >2 L/min) created in a pa-
tient with established heart failure may worsen the
pre-existing heart failure.
It is useful to have a baseline cardiac 2D echo to inform
the operator because it may inﬂuence the type or location
of vascular access and provide the nephrologist with a
reference in case the patient develops future signs or
symptoms of heart failure. Indeed, when otherwise stable
HD patients develop signs or symptoms of heart failure,
the nephrologist should consider the AV access as a po-
tential contributing factor.
The use of routine cardiac 2D echo in addition to vigilant
monitoring of signs and symptoms of heart failure may be
helpful in detecting high ﬂow cardiac failure. High ﬂow
cardiac output has been associated with Qa/CO > 20%.
There are other 2D echo parameters used to guide the
monitoring and management of high ﬂow AV access such
as right ventricular longitudinal strain. As discussed previ-
ously, ﬂow reduction techniques and possibly AV access
ligation may be necessary. Such important discussions must
be made together with the patient and the dialysis and
vascular access teams. The discussion should include alter-
native access treatments and potential future kidney
replacement modalities and associated accesses because the
loss of an AV access is the loss of one of the patient’s
lifelines for HD.
