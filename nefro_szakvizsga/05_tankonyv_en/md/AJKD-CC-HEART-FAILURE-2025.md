Kidney Dysfunction in Heart Failure: Core
Curriculum 2025
Kevin Bryan Lo, Arun Janakiraman, and Janani Rangaswami
The pathophysiology of heart failure (HF) with kidney dysfunction is represented by several mal-
adaptive bidirectional pathways wherein acute or chronic dysfunction of one organ drives acute or
chronic dysfunction in the other organ. Suboptimal decongestion, diuretic resistance, and low use
rates of guideline-directed medical therapy in individuals with kidney dysfunction and HF contribute to
poor cardiovascular and kidney outcomes. Recent developments with the early identiﬁcation and
treatment of diuretic resistance may help mitigate the harmful effects of persistent congestion in in-
dividuals with HF. Several classes of guideline-directed medical therapies in HF have been shown to
reduce death, hospitalizations for HF, and kidney function decline and improve quality of life. A
combination of efﬁcient and personalized strategies to achieve decongestion while optimizing the
implementation of evidence-based therapies that modify the trajectory of HF are essential in tandem to
reduce adverse outcomes and premature death in this high-risk patient population.

In this individual, the blood pressure is high and there is
clinical evidence of preserved perfusion. Increased right-

kidney function in HF across the ejection fraction spectrum;
therefore, (a) is the best answer to Question 1. This has
been demonstrated in animal models of kidney venous
clamping and human studies using invasive hemodynamic

ciated with worsening kidney function in patients with HF
independent of cardiac output. It is helpful to conceive the
net effect of cardiac output and right-sided venous pressures
on kidney perfusion using the construct of “kidney perfu-
sion pressure,” which is quantiﬁed clinically as the differ-
ence between mean arterial pressure and central venous
pressure (ideally >60 mm Hg), with disruption of either of
these elements critically reducing kidney perfusion in HF.
Mechanisms mediating worsening kidney function in
patients with venous hypertension include increased kidney
interstitial pressure, increased intratubular pressure, and
RAAS/SNS activation, which increases sodium reabsorption,
thereby perpetuating congestion. Kidney venous hyperten-
sion increases hydrostatic pressures comparably in the per-
itubular capillaries as well as in the interstitium, resulting in
a net even effect with respect to Starling forces between
these compartments. However, increased kidney interstitial
pressure enhances lymphatic outﬂow, promoting washout
of proteins, signiﬁcantly reducing colloidal osmotic pressure
in the kidney interstitium, which further facilitates sodium
reabsorption. In parallel, colloidal osmotic pressure in-
creases in the peritubular capillaries because of the increased
ﬁltration fraction in the nephron to maintain the glomerular
ﬁltration rate (GFR) in the setting of reduced kidney blood
ﬂow (the ﬁltration fraction is the percentage of blood
ﬁltered by the glomerulus; GFR divided by renal plasma
ﬂow). The resultant increase in sodium and water absorp-
tion in the proximal convoluted tubule reduces distal de-
livery of sodium in HF, which has important therapeutic
implications given the sites of action for loop and thiazide
diuretic agents. In states of reduced kidney blood ﬂow (due
to declining cardiac output), GFR can be preserved initially
by a compensatory increase in ﬁltration fraction from
efferent arteriolar constriction as a result of increased renin
levels in acute HF. When there has been frank and persistent
reduction in cardiac output, glomerular ﬁltration is reduced,
driven by angiotensin II–mediated constriction of the
afferent and efferent arterioles.
Apart from mean arterial pressure and central venous
pressure, other mechanisms have been identiﬁed that
contribute to kidney dysfunction. Increased intraabdominal
pressures can lead to compression of renal veins and ureters,
leading to overall reductions in glomerular ﬁltration similar
to the mechanisms of kidney venous congestion described
above. There are emerging data on the role of the kidney’s
capsule in causing “kidney tamponade,” thereby contrib-
uting to reduced kidney perfusion pressure. Maldistribution
of volume in the splanchnic circulation, which is mobilized
in the setting of venoconstriction in HF (a concept called
stress volume), also contributes to increased ﬁlling pres-
sures. Endothelial dysfunction and inﬂammatory pathways
(including via alterations in the gut microbiome) may also
contribute to worsening kidney function in HF. Abnor-
malities in the microvasculature including reduced nitric
oxide and oxidative stress can lead to endothelial damage,
promoting
downstream
inﬂammation
with
eventual
endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition and ﬁbrosis (Fig 1).
Maladaptive RAAS and SNS activity is upstream and
central to these cardiac–kidney interactions and generates
a persistent sodium-avid state in patients with HF. This
underscores the importance of addressing not only
decongestion and its challenges (ie, diuretic resistance)
in HF, but the early need to optimize the neurohor-
monal system in HF as a disease-modifying target,
including and especially in individuals with concomitant
kidney dysfunction.

diuretic response during decongestion for hospital-
ized HF?
(a) Daily measurement of patient weight
(b) Urine sodium concentration measured 2 hours after

(c) Charted 24-hour urine output
(d) Clinical signs and symptoms assessed at the bedside

For the answer to this question, see the following text.
Daily weights and urine output measurements in acute
HF are affected by myriad patient- and systems-related
factors, making them relatively unreliable in measuring
diuretic response adequacy. Furthermore, these measures
take time (≥1 day for weight change) to be able to observe
a trend or a signiﬁcant change. Clinical signs and symp-
toms are difﬁcult to accurately ascertain. As mentioned
above, acute HF is a highly sodium-avid state driven by
excess and maladaptive neurohormonal responses (via the
RAAS and SNS) to the failing heart. Therefore, the degree
of sodium avidity can potentially reﬂect the degree of
diuretic resistance and the efﬁcacy of natriuresis achieved
by diuretic therapy and quickly help in ascertaining
necessary changes for diuretic agent dosing; therefore, (b)
is the best answer to Question 3. The use of a spot urine
sodium measurement 2 hours after the ﬁrst diuretic dose is
a reliable way of predicting subsequent 6-hour natriuresis,
as validated by the natriuretic response prediction equa-
tion. A spot urine sodium concentration <50-70 mEq/L at
2 hours after loop diuretic agent administration or an
hourly urine output <100-150 mL during the ﬁrst 6 hours
denotes an insufﬁcient diuretic response. This can allow
decision-making for rapid diuretic agent uptitration to
improve natriuresis. A single-center prospective pragmatic
randomized clinical trial (the PUSH-AHF trial) explored

Diuretic Resistance and Enhanced Decongestive
Strategies
Case 4: A 53-year-old woman with a history of HF with
preserved EF with an EF of 55%, diabetes, hypertension, and
chronic kidney disease (CKD; KDIGO [Kidney Disease:
Improving Global Outcomes] stage G4 A3) is hospitalized
with an HF exacerbation. Outpatient medications include
lisinopril, metoprolol succinate, dapagliﬂozin, and torsemide
(80 mg) daily. Blood pressure is 110/70 mm Hg, heart rate is
80 bpm, respiratory rate is 24 breaths/min, and temperature
is 37.3C. Initial laboratory studies show a sodium concen-
tration of 132 mEq/L, potassium concentration of 4.5 mEq/L,
chloride concentration of 92 mEq/L, serum creatinine con-
centration of 3.1 mg/dL (at baseline), SUN concentration of
55 mg/dL, and NT-proBNP concentration of 5,000 pg/mL.
The patient undergoes diuresis with intravenous furosemide
200 mg twice daily, but, during the ﬁrst 24 hours, the net
intake/output status is +300 mL. There was no noted weight
change in 24 hours. The patient continues to receive 4-6 L of

Question 4: What is the next best step for this patient?
(a) Increase furosemide to 400 mg intravenously twice daily
(b) Change diuretic agent to bumetanide 4 mg intravenously
twice daily
(c) Add metolazone 5 mg/d
(d) Discontinue lisinopril treatment
(e) Initiate ultraﬁltration
For the answer to this question, see the following text.
This patient has diuretic resistance, deﬁned as the
attenuation of the maximal diuretic effect that ultimately
limits sodium and chloride excretion. Related to this is the
concept of diuretic efﬁciency, which refers to the net ﬂuid
lost/urine output per milligram of loop diuretic agent
(usually in per–40-mg furosemide dose equivalents).
Diuretic resistance is associated with poor outcomes such
as worsening kidney function, recurrent HF hospitaliza-
tions, and mortality. The patient has advanced CKD,
which, in itself, may contribute to diuretic resistance, even
though diuretic resistance is usually not entirely explained
by CKD alone. Accumulation of organic anions that
compete for diuretic secretion in the proximal convoluted
tubule, and overall diminished ﬁltered load of sodium
from a decreased GFR from critically reduced nephron
mass in more advanced CKD, are mechanisms via which
CKD can contribute to diuretic resistance. Notably, this
patient is already receiving high doses of an oral diuretic
medication. Generally, the natriuretic response to each
subsequent dose decreases, constituting a phenomenon
called diuretic braking. This results from the combined
hyperactivity of the RAAS and SNS. There is also nephron
remodeling, particularly distal tubular hypertrophy that
increases distal sodium reabsorption and aldosterone-
triggered responses in the collecting duct with increased
epithelial sodium channel–mediated reabsorption of so-
dium. This redistribution of sodium delivery and reab-
sorption is also seen with sodium glucose cotransporter
inhibitors, as has been demonstrated elegantly using
lithium excretion studies. Hypochloremia and metabolic
alkalosis both antagonize the effects of loop diuretic agents
by reducing the intraluminal chloride gradient and trig-
gering the adaptive neurohormonal responses described
above.
Pendrin is a chloride-bicarbonate anion exchanger
located in the collecting ducts that plays a major role in
chloride reabsorption from the tubules in exchange for
bicarbonate secretion. Pendrin acts in concert with the
sodium-dependent chloride and bicarbonate exchanger
transport that cotransports sodium and bicarbonate for
reabsorption in exchange for chloride secretion. This

Various approaches have been attempted, including the
concept of sequential nephron blockade to achieve “enhanced
decongestion” as an outcome in acute HF. This approach uses
different diuretic agents acting across nephron segments to
help augment the overall diuretic response and bypass the
previously mentioned adaptive mechanisms; therefore, (c) is
the best answer to Question 4. Small studies have shown the
efﬁcacy of the addition of thiazide-type diuretic agents to help
augment the overall diuretic response. However, until
recently, combinations of different diuretic agent types were
not studied in larger randomized trials. The CLOROTIC trial
randomized 230 hospitalized patients with acute HF to
receive hydrochlorothiazide or placebo in addition to furo-
semide standard therapy. Patients receiving hydrochlorothia-
zide had greater weight loss and volume of diuresis at the
expense of greater rates of impaired kidney function. It is
important to clarify that the impaired kidney function in
this trial is of uncertain clinical signiﬁcance because no
assessment of tubular injury was performed, and the
overall clinical outcomes remained neutral. The ADVOR
randomized trial examined acetazolamide, a carbonic
anhydrase inhibitor that predominantly acts in the
proximal convoluted tubule reducing sodium reabsorp-
tion versus placebo, in addition to standard loop diuretic
therapies in acute HF with varied EFs. This trial ran-
domized 519 patients hospitalized with HF to receive
acetazolamide intravenously once daily or placebo in
addition to their intravenous loop diuretic regimen. The
main end points of successful decongestion (deﬁned by
absence of signs of volume overload within 3 days after
randomization) was higher in the acetazolamide group
than in placebo group (42.2% vs 30.5%). There were also
greater rates of natriuresis and urine volume documented
in the acetazolamide group. However, no differences
between groups were noted for hard clinical end points
such as hospitalizations for HF, mortality, or quality of
life.

trial. This trial randomized 530 patients hospitalized with
HF (with varying EFs) to receive empagliﬂozin or placebo

of a hierarchical composite “win ratio” of death, HF ex-

Other trials testing add-on therapies such as ATHENA-

HF did not show beneﬁts in terms of hard clinical out-

aquaretic agent, whose mechanism of action is a vaso-
pressin V2 receptor antagonist leading to predominantly
water excretion. There are not enough head-to-head
comparative studies comparing these agents used for
sequential nephron blockade. In a small randomized trial
(3T trial) of patients with diuretic resistance that compared
oral metolazone, intravenous chlorothiazide, and tolvaptan
added to standard high-dose furosemide, weight loss was
similar across all 3 strategies, with improved overall
diuretic efﬁcacy (Table 1).
The use of hypertonic saline solution for decongestion
in HF, although seemingly counterintuitive, has recently
gained traction, with the theoretical approach of disrup-
tion of the sodium-avidity signal tied to neurohormonal
activation. Some studies have shown an improvement in
urine output and diuretic efﬁciency among patients with
acute decompensated HF with administration of hyper-
tonic saline solution with high doses of loop diuretic
agents. However, high-quality evidence is still lacking for
this approach, but it can be potentially considered in re-
fractory cases, especially in patients who are poor candi-
dates for ultraﬁltration.
So far, most trials (including the recent ones empha-
sizing enhanced decongestion strategies) did not select pa-
tients with or at high risk for diuretic resistance, but instead
enrolled patients with acute HF in general. Among the
contemporary trials, the CLOROTIC trial enrolled patients at
higher risk for diuretic resistance, given a baseline diuretic
agent use of 80-240 mg furosemide equivalents and an
estimated GFR (eGFR) range of 32-58 ml/min/1.73 m2,
and struggled with slow enrollment, reaching a sample size
of only 230 patients. To this end, the wider availability and
validation of early detection of diuretic resistance using
sodium output is helpful in informing patient selection in
future trials for the optimization of decongestion strategies.

The treatment and management of HF (with a reduced,
mildly reduced, or preserved EF) has evolved signiﬁcantly
over time through numerous trials. Based on current data,
GDMT for HFrEF involves the use of the following classes
of medication: β-adrenergic receptor blockers, RAAS in-
hibitors (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, ARBs,
or ARN inhibitors), SGLT2 inhibitors, and MRAs; there-
fore, (b) is the best answer to Question 7. These medi-
cation classes constitute the “pillars of therapy” in HFrEF,
and, when used in tandem and titrated quickly to reach
target goals, have a mortality beneﬁt, along with reduced
hospitalizations for HF, as recently demonstrated in the
pragmatic STRONG HF trial.
β-Adrenergic receptor blockers in HFrEF are used
widely across the range of CKD stages with the least
therapeutic inertia, likely because they are eGFR-neutral.
Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and ARBs have
been shown to have mortality and morbidity beneﬁts
mainly in the HFrEF (EF <40%) population, as seen in the
previous landmark trials (CONSENSUS, SOLVD, Val-HeFT;
Table 2); however, they tend to be underused in HFrEF
with CKD because of concerns for hyperkalemia and acute
kidney injury. The ARN inhibitor sacubitril/valsartan has
demonstrated important clinical beneﬁts in cardiovascular
and/or kidney outcomes in patients with HFrEF and HF
with a mildly reduced EF. Neprilysin is an endopeptidase
that cleaves a variety of peptides such as natriuretic peptides,
bradykinin, adrenomedullin, substance P, angiotensin I and
II, and endothelin. It has a broad role in several cardiovas-
cular, kidney, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, endocrine, and
neurologic functions. The combined ARN inhibitor has
been developed with an intent to increase vasodilatory
natriuretic peptides and prevent counterregulatory activa-
tion of the angiotensin system. When comparing the use of
ARN inhibitors versus ARBs for HF with preserved EF, the
PARAGON-HF study (Prospective Comparison of ARNI
with ARB Global Outcomes in HF with preserved EF)
found that sacubitril/valsartan was associated with a
lower eGFR slope decline and decreased HF-related
hospitalizations compared with valsartan alone. Sacubi-

and all-cause mortality in HFrEF versus enalapril in the
PARADIGM-HF trial (Prospective Comparison of ARNI
with ACE-I to Determine Impact on Global Mortality and
Morbidity in Heart Failure).

Changes in kidney function in the setting of HF manage-
ment have to be interpreted in the context of decongestion and
GDMT initiation/uptitration. Worsening kidney function,
especially in the setting of aggressive decongestion, is
frequently encountered. However, these changes in GFR may
not reﬂect true tubular injury (Fig 7). In fact, in the setting of
acute decompensated HF, worsening kidney function in the
setting of successful decongestion is associated with lower
mortality and reduced hospitalization rates than failure to
decongest in the setting of stable or improving kidney func-
tion. Several caveats exist in interpreting changes in markers of
glomerular function (especially serum creatinine) in the acute
HF state and in patients with sarcopenia with HF. This makes
serum creatinine particularly problematic for an accurate
assessment of eGFR in patients with HF. Therefore, moving
away from a siloed interpretation of ﬂuctuating markers of
glomerular ﬁltration for prognostication toward recognizing
persistent congestion and deploying a targeted approach to
treating diuretic resistance may help improve outcomes in
patients with HF. This is an area for future research in clinical
trials. Initiation of GDMT for HF can also contribute to ﬂuc-
tuations in kidney function. Initiation of a RAAS inhibitor or
SGLT2 inhibitor can be associated with ﬂuctuations in serum
creatinine of as much as 0.3 mg/dL or more within the ﬁrst 2
weeks of initiation. Guidelines have recommended continua-
tion if the eGFR decrease is <30% versus baseline. In a pre-
speciﬁed analysis of the EMPA-KIDNEY trial, although there
was an approximately 6% decrease in eGFR in the ﬁrst 2
months, over time, the chronic slope of eGFR decline was
halved in the empagliﬂozin group compared with the placebo
group, showing slowing of kidney disease progression with a
relative difference of 50% (95% CI, 42%-58%). Continued use
of GDMT in patients with HF and CKD is associated with
improvement in cardiac and kidney outcomes, and the deﬁ-
nition of stage 1 acute kidney injury by standard criteria do not
apply to the hemodynamic context of decongestion and
GDMT use in HF; therefore, (a) is the best answer to Question
9. It is important to carefully ascertain if there is true tubular
injury in the appropriate clinical context (eg, sepsis, bleeding),
and urine microscopy is a valuable tool in this regard, with
excellent negative predictive value for a diagnosis of clinically
important intrinsic kidney injury.
